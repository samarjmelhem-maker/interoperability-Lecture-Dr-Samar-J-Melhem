Why Interoperability Matters in Pharmacy

The gap between "digital records" and "useful, shareable data" — and why pharmacists live in that gap.

The three-letter soup: EMR, EHR, PHR

Before we talk about systems speaking to each other, we need precise vocabulary for the systems themselves. These three terms sound similar but differ on who owns the record, how far it travels, and what it is allowed to do.

Electronic Medical Record (EMR) — a digital version of the paper chart used inside a single organisation. It is owned and controlled by the provider/facility and rarely leaves it intact.

Electronic Health Record (EHR) — a longitudinal health record designed to be shared across organisations by authorised clinicians. This is the record category where interoperability lives.

Personal Health Record (PHR) — a health record owned and controlled by the patient, often through a mobile app or portal. Pharmacists verify and reconcile PHR data rather than trust it automatically.

🏥
EMR — "My Record"

Single clinic's chart. If the patient changes hospital, most of the chart does not travel with them.

🌐
EHR — "Everyone's Record"

Designed for multi-organisation sharing — the backbone of safe transitions of care.

📱
PHR — "Patient's Record"

Patient-controlled. Excellent engagement tool; data needs clinical verification.

Mnemonic: EMR = one org · EHR = everyone (with permission) · PHR = patient. If the question says "patient-entered" or "self-tracked", it's PHR. If it says "shared across settings", it's EHR.

What is interoperability, really?

Interoperability — the ability of different information systems, devices, and applications to access, exchange, integrate, and cooperatively use data in a coordinated manner, within and across organisational, regional and national boundaries, to provide timely and seamless portability of information and optimise the health of individuals and populations.
Adapted from the HIMSS / ISO definition.

Read that definition slowly. Three verbs matter: access, exchange, integrate and use. A hospital can exchange a PDF with a community pharmacy and still fail interoperability — because a PDF cannot be integrated and used by a dispensing system to trigger a duplicate-therapy alert.

The pharmacist's 4 transition risks when interoperability fails

1
Duplicate therapy

Glucophage XR 500 mg and Metformin HCl 500 mg ER arrive as two different text strings — the system treats them as two drugs.

2
Missed allergy alerts

"Allergic to PCN" as free text cannot match penicillin orders at the ADR rule level.

3
Wrong renal dose

Outside-lab serum creatinine delayed or with mismatched units — eGFR falls out of automated dose-adjust calculators.

4
Stuck e-prescriptions

NewRx arrives with a missing field; the Rx queues for manual fix, delaying dispensing.

CHECK 1 Quick recall

A patient hands her pharmacist a medication list from her mobile tracker app. The list disagrees with the hospital discharge summary. Which record type is the app?

Correct: C. A patient-controlled mobile tracker is a PHR. Your clinical move: reconcile — don't automatically trust or automatically discard. PHRs often reveal adherence patterns and OTC use the EHR never captured, but strengths, dose forms, and frequencies may be self-reported inaccurately.

The Four Levels of Interoperability

A ladder, not a menu. Each rung answers a different question — and each rung can fail independently.

Progressive questions, progressive levels

Use these four questions in order. A "no" at any rung tells you exactly where the problem lives.

1
Foundational — "Can we exchange data at all?"

Basic secure connectivity. A web service answers; a file transfer completes. No guarantee the bytes are meaningful yet.

2
Structural — "Can the receiver parse it?"

Agreed message format, syntax, data types, units. HL7 v2 segments, XML schemas, JSON profiles, UCUM units all live here.

3
Semantic — "Do we agree on what it means?"

Shared terminologies guarantee that "serum creatinine" means the same test and "penicillin allergy" means the same substance + reaction on both sides.

4
Organisational — "Are we permitted to do this, and who governs it?"

Consent, policy, legal framework, trust agreements, data-use agreements, governance committees — the non-technical layer that decides whether the exchange is allowed.

Exam hook: if the scenario describes same field, different interpretation, it's semantic. If it describes data exists but wrong format or wrong units, it's structural. If it's about consent or who is allowed to see what, it's organisational. If nothing connects at all, it's foundational.

CHECK 2 Where does this failure live?

A lab result arrives at the hospital EHR. The LOINC code is correct (2160-0). However, the sending system used units of µmol/L while the hospital configured its alert engine for mg/dL. The "critically high" alerts never fire.

Correct: B — Structural. The semantic layer agrees (same test, LOINC 2160-0). The breakdown is in representation: how the numeric value and its units are packaged. Fix = enforce UCUM units at the interface and add a unit-conversion step. This is why integration testing must validate not only codes but units, reference ranges, precision and datatypes.

Terminology #1 — RxNorm (medication normalisation)

RxNorm — a standardised nomenclature for clinical drugs maintained by the U.S. National Library of Medicine. It assigns each medication concept an RxCUI (RxNorm Concept Unique Identifier) and links brand names, generics, strengths, and dose forms to one concept.

The problem RxNorm solves

Three systems receive the same prescription in three different strings:

Before RxNorm — same drug, three strings
# Pharmacy A sends: "Glucophage XR 500mg extended-release tablet" # Hospital LIS sends: "Metformin hydrochloride 500mg ER tablet" # Clinic EMR sends: "Metformin HCl 500mg XR tab" # Receiving system: treats them as 3 different medications ⚠️1
Sticky note
1
Why this is dangerous in pharmacy. If three records refer to the same molecule but the system thinks they are three different drugs, the patient appears to be on three concurrent metformin prescriptions. The duplicate-therapy alert should fire — but it cannot, because the system never recognises them as duplicates. This is exactly how patients end up on accidental double-dosing. The fix is to give every drug one canonical identifier.
After RxNorm normalisation — one RxCUI
RxCUI2: 860975 # metformin hydrochloride 500 MG Extended Release Oral Tablet TTY3: SCD # Semantic Clinical Drug Name: "metformin hydrochloride 500 MG Extended Release Oral Tablet" # All 3 strings → same RxCUI → duplicate-therapy alert fires ✓
Sticky notes
2
RxCUI = "RxNorm Concept Unique Identifier" — a number that names a drug concept unambiguously, the way an ISBN names a book. Every drug, every strength, every form, every brand has its own RxCUI. 860975 always means the same thing, in any system that uses RxNorm. It is free to use and maintained by the US National Library of Medicine.
3
TTY = "Term Type" — RxNorm classifies each concept by what level of specificity it carries: IN (just the ingredient), SCD (ingredient + strength + form, generic), SBD (same but branded), BN (just the brand name). When designing a duplicate-therapy alert, you usually compare at the IN level (so amoxicillin 500 mg and amoxicillin 250 mg both flag as duplicates).

RxNorm term types (TTYs) — the ones pharmacists actually meet

Ingredient + strength + dose form (generic). Metformin 500 MG ER Oral Tablet.

GET1 https://rxnav.nlm.nih.gov/REST/rxcui.json?name=metformin%20500mg%20ER2 # Response (abbreviated): { "idGroup": { "name": "metformin 500mg ER", "rxnormId": ["860975"]3 } }
Sticky notes — what is an "API"?
1
"GET" means "please fetch me this information". An API ("Application Programming Interface") is just a structured way for one piece of software to ask another for data — like dialling a specific extension on an automated phone system. You don't need to write code to use one: paste this URL into a browser and you'll get the same answer back.
2
The URL is the question. Read it like a sentence: "Go to rxnav.nlm.nih.gov, ask the REST endpoint rxcui, give me JSON, and the search term is metformin 500mg ER". The %20 is just URL-speak for a space. Try opening this URL in your browser — it works without any login.
3
The answer comes back as JSON. Reading from the inside out: the rxnormId is a list (square brackets) containing the answer "860975". That single number is the canonical RxCUI for "metformin 500mg ER" — the same identifier you'd put into your EHR's MedicationRequest, your DUR engine, your prior-authorisation form, anywhere a drug needs to be uniquely named.

That single identifier — 860975 — is now portable across every system that maps to RxNorm. No more fuzzy string matching.

If the scenario says "same drug, different names" or "brand vs generic on reconciliation" — reach for RxNorm.

Terminology #2 — SNOMED CT (comprehensive clinical terminology)

SNOMED CT (Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine — Clinical Terms) is the world's most comprehensive clinical terminology. It covers clinical findings, disorders, procedures, body structures, organisms, substances, and relationships between them — with over 350,000 concepts.

Each SNOMED CT concept has:

  • a unique numeric SCTID (e.g., 91936005);
  • a Fully Specified Name with a semantic tag (e.g., Allergy to penicillin (finding));
  • one or more descriptions (preferred term + synonyms); and
  • logical relationships to parent and related concepts (e.g., Is-a, Causative agent, Finding site).
SNOMED CT concept — Allergy to penicillin
Concept ID1: 91936005 FSN2: "Allergy to penicillin (finding)" Preferred: "Penicillin allergy" # Relationships: Is-a3 → 416098002 "Drug allergy (finding)" Causative agent4 → 373270004 "Penicillin -class of antibiotic- (substance)" Finding site → 39057004 "Pulmonary valve structure" # example relation
Sticky notes — SNOMED CT structure
1
Concept ID = SCTID — every SNOMED concept has its own permanent number. 91936005 means "Allergy to penicillin" forever, in every language, in every country that uses SNOMED. The number itself has no meaning — you cannot decode "what kind of concept" it is from the digits alone — but the structure of the concept is described by its relationships.
2
FSN = "Fully Specified Name". This is the unambiguous English name. The text in brackets at the end — (finding) — is the semantic tag. It tells you what kind of concept this is: a finding (clinical observation), a disorder, a procedure, a substance, a body structure, etc. Penicillin the molecule is also in SNOMED but tagged (substance) with a different SCTID. Always read the bracketed tag.
3
"Is-a" = the parent in the hierarchy. Penicillin allergy is-a drug allergy is-a hypersensitivity reaction is-a finding. This hierarchy is what allows a query "show all drug allergies" to retrieve every kind of drug allergy automatically — you don't have to enumerate them. SNOMED CT's hierarchy has hundreds of thousands of these is-a links, all curated.
4
"Causative agent" is a relationship that links this allergy concept to what causes it — penicillin the substance class. This is what enables a smart pharmacy alert: when you order amoxicillin (an RxNorm drug), the system maps amoxicillin to its SNOMED substance concept, walks up to "penicillin -class of antibiotic-" via SNOMED relationships, and recognises the patient's documented allergy applies. Without this link, the alert would only fire on the exact same drug.

How a pharmacist uses SNOMED CT today

🚨
Coding allergies & ADRs

Substance + reaction manifestation + severity as coded concepts — not free text.

📋
Structured clinical findings

"Nausea", "dyspnoea", "wheeze" — each has an SCTID that travels safely.

🧪
Procedures & interventions

"Medication review", "INR monitoring" — pharmacist activities coded for reporting.

🔗
Linking meanings across systems

The same SCTID in the hospital, the clinic, and the community pharmacy means the same thing — by design.

🌐 Try the real SNOMED CT browser▾

Anyone can query SNOMED CT for free. The official international browser lives at browser.ihtsdotools.org and the UK NHS mirror at termbrowser.nhs.uk. Type "penicillin allergy" and you'll land on concept 91936005 with its hierarchy, synonyms, and relationships — the same data every conforming EHR would use to drive its allergy-alert engine.

If the scenario describes an allergic reaction, adverse drug reaction, symptom, finding, or clinical procedure — SNOMED CT is usually the right terminology.

Terminology #3 — LOINC (labs, vitals, observations)

LOINC (Logical Observation Identifiers Names and Codes) provides a universal code system for identifying laboratory tests, clinical measurements, and other observations. Maintained by the Regenstrief Institute. Each code names a test by six formal axes: Component, Property, Timing, System/Specimen, Scale, and (sometimes) Method.

LOINC 2160-0 — fully specified
LOINC code: 2160-01 Long name: "Creatinine [Mass/volume] in Serum or Plasma" Short name: "Creat SerPl-mCnc" # Six-axis specification:2 Component: Creatinine Property: MCnc # Mass concentration Time: Pt # Point in time (spot/random) System: Ser/Plas3 # Serum or plasma Scale: Qn # Quantitative Method: (none specified) Example UCUM units4: mg/dL # or µmol/L — must match receiver config
Sticky notes — what each LOINC field means
1
The LOINC code 2160-0 is just an identifier — its job is to point at one specific test. The number-dash-number format is LOINC's signature; everything before the dash is the actual code, the digit after the dash is a check digit (a kind of self-test that catches typos). You don't need to memorise the number, just recognise the format.
2
The "six axes" are LOINC's clever idea: instead of giving each test a vague name, LOINC describes every test along six dimensions — what is being measured, how, when, on what specimen, on what scale, by what method. Two tests that look identical but differ on even one axis get different codes. This is why LOINC is safe for interoperability: a urine creatinine and a serum creatinine cannot be confused.
3
The "System" axis = the specimen. Ser/Plas means serum or plasma; Urine means urine; Bld means whole blood. This is the most important axis for safety. A renal-dose adjustment algorithm written for serum creatinine MUST refuse to act on urine creatinine — the numbers are completely different and would cause catastrophic errors.
4
UCUM = "Unified Code for Units of Measure". It's the partner standard to LOINC: LOINC says what is being measured, UCUM says in what units. A LOINC code without a UCUM unit is meaningless. UCUM is case-sensitive: mg/dL is correct; mg/dl, MG/DL, Mg/dL are all rejected by conformant systems. Many real-world interoperability incidents come from this case sensitivity.

The six-axis structure is why LOINC is safe for interoperability: two tests that differ even in specimen or timing get different codes. "Urine creatinine" and "serum creatinine" are not the same LOINC — and a dosing algorithm must not confuse them.

Terminology #4 — ICD-10 / ICD-11 (classification, not clinical depth)

ICD (International Classification of Diseases, WHO) groups diagnoses into statistical categories for billing, reimbursement, prior authorisation, and epidemiology. ICD-11 — the current edition — is the first born-digital ICD, with an API and improved handling of multiple morbidity.

Key distinction students confuse: ICD is a classification, SNOMED CT is a clinical terminology. ICD gives you the billable category. SNOMED CT gives you the clinical nuance. Modern EHRs store both and map between them.

If the scenario mentions prior authorisation, insurance billing, or population-level reporting → think ICD. If it's clinical detail, allergy reaction, or exact finding → think SNOMED CT.

Transport #1 — HL7 v2.x (the pipe-delimited workhorse)

HL7 v2 — the dominant hospital messaging standard for 30+ years. Human-readable but pipe-heavy. Organised into segments (3-letter codes like MSH, PID, OBX) separated by carriage returns, with fields separated by |, components by ^, and sub-components by &.

Here is a real-ish HL7 v2.5.1 lab result message (ORU^R01) carrying a serum creatinine on a Jordanian patient. Click the yellow pins to decode each part — HL7 v2 looks intimidating but it is just a sentence with strict punctuation:

MSH1|^~\&2|JUH_LAB|JUH|EHR|JUH|2026042410303||ORU^R014|MSG0001|P|2.5.1 PID5|1||JUH-9988776||Nabulsi^Ahmad^M7||19580612|M|||Amman^^Amman^^11118^JO OBR8|1||LAB2026-0456|2160-0^Creatinine [Mass/volume] in Ser/Plas^LN OBX9|1|NM10|2160-0^Creatinine^LN11||1.8212|mg/dL13|0.7-1.3|H14||F|||202604241028
Sticky notes — reading HL7 v2
1
Segments are the lines. Every HL7 v2 message is a stack of lines, each starting with a 3-letter code that names the line's purpose. MSH = Message Header, PID = Patient ID, OBR = Observation Request (what was ordered), OBX = Observation Result (the answer). Think of the message like a paper lab report: the header tells you which lab sent it, then patient demographics, then the test ordered, then the result.
2
The funny characters |^~\& are HL7's punctuation, declared up front. Every other line uses these same separators: | = field separator, ^ = component separator (smaller than a field, like name parts), ~ = repetition, \ = escape, & = sub-component. They look messy but they are like commas in a CSV file — once you know their job, you can parse anything.
3
Timestamp 202604241030 reads as 2026-04-24, 10:30. HL7 v2 uses one continuous string of digits with no separators: YYYYMMDDHHMM. This is the time the message was created by the sender (the lab), not the time the test was performed.
4
ORU^R01 = the message type. ORU means "Observational Result, Unsolicited" — the lab pushes the result without being asked. R01 is the trigger event ("a new result is available"). Other types you'll meet: ADT^A01 (admission), RDS^O13 (pharmacy dispense), OMP^O09 (pharmacy order). The receiving system uses this code to decide which workflow to fire.
5
PID = Patient Identification. Everything on this line is about who the patient is: ID number, name, birthdate, sex, address. There is exactly one PID per patient. If a message arrives with mismatched PID and OBX (e.g., wrong patient), that is the most dangerous error in clinical messaging — the right result attached to the wrong patient.
6
JUH-998877 is Ahmad's hospital MRN — Jordan University Hospital's local identifier. In a real message, this field also carries the assigning authority (who issued the ID) and the identifier type (MR = medical record, NI = national ID, etc.). Two hospitals can have a patient with MRN 998877 meaning two completely different people — that's why the assigning authority matters.
7
Nabulsi^Ahmad^M shows how the caret ^ separates name components: family^given^middle. Reads as: family name "Nabulsi", given name "Ahmad", middle initial "M". This is HL7 v2's compact way of carrying a full name in one field.
8
OBR = Observation Request. This says what was ordered — here, a serum creatinine, identified by LOINC code 2160-0. There is one OBR per ordered test (you'd see multiple OBR segments if a panel was ordered).
9
OBX = Observation Result. This is where the actual result lives. One OBX per result. A complete metabolic panel would have ~14 OBX segments — one per analyte. The structure is always: which test, what type of value (number, text, coded), the value itself, units, reference range, abnormal flag, status.
10
NM = the result is a numeric value. Other common types: ST (string text), CE/CWE (coded entry, e.g., for cultures), SN (structured numeric, like "<0.1"). The receiving system uses this type to decide how to display and validate the value.
11
2160-0^Creatinine^LN — same LOINC code as in OBR, repeated here to confirm "the result you're about to see is for this test". The ^LN at the end declares the code system as LOINC. If OBR-4 and OBX-3 do not match, the message is malformed.
12
1.82 — the numeric result. Always read it together with the next two fields (units and reference range) — a number on its own is meaningless.
13
mg/dL — the unit. Should be a valid UCUM unit (case-sensitive: mg/dL is correct; MG/DL or mg/dl are not). The downstream renal-dose engine string-matches this token; one wrong character and the alert silently fails to fire.
14
H = High — the abnormal flag. Other values: L (low), HH (critically high), LL (critically low), N (normal), A (abnormal but unspecified). For a pharmacist, the H flag is the trigger to check the patient's medication list for nephrotoxic or renally-excreted drugs.

Reading it one segment at a time

SegmentNameWhat it carries
MSHMessage HeaderSender, receiver, message type (ORU^R01 = unsolicited lab result), version
PIDPatient IdentificationMRN, name, DOB, sex, address — the patient
OBRObservation RequestWhat was ordered (creatinine)

Pharmacist lens: the OBX flag "H" triggers the renal-dose alert engine. If the interface engine drops the OBX-8 flag during translation, the alert never fires — a silent interoperability failure that can harm patients on aminoglycosides, vancomycin, metformin, direct oral anticoagulants and dozens more.

Transport #2 — NCPDP SCRIPT (the e-prescribing standard)

NCPDP SCRIPT — the National Council for Prescription Drug Programs standard for electronic prescribing between prescribers and pharmacies. XML-based. Defines message types including NewRx (new prescription), RxRenewal, RxChange, CancelRx, RxFill (dispense notification), and RxHistory (medication history query).

The required elements of a complete NewRx message map onto the "five rights": right patient, right drug, right dose/directions, right prescriber, right pharmacy. Miss any one field and the message either fails validation (structural failure) or queues for manual rework.

👤
Patient

Name, DOB, address, phone, gender, identifier

💊
Medication

RxNorm-coded drug, strength, dose form, quantity, days supply, refills

📋
Directions (SIG)

Structured SIG — verb, dose, route, frequency, indication

👨‍⚕️
Prescriber

Name, DEA/NPI or equivalent, contact, signature

🏪
Pharmacy

Recipient identifier (NCPDP ID or equivalent)

🕐
Metadata

Date written, message IDs, DUR screening flags

When you see a scenario where "the e-prescription arrives missing a field" — that's NCPDP SCRIPT, and the problem is structural interoperability. The semantic layer may be fine (the drug was RxNorm-coded); the envelope is incomplete.

Transport #3 — HL7 FHIR (where the future is already arriving)

FHIR (Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources) is the modern HL7 standard: a set of modular resources (Patient, MedicationRequest, Observation, AllergyIntolerance, …) exchanged over standard REST APIs as JSON or XML. It is the backbone of mobile health apps, patient-facing portals, and SMART-on-FHIR apps that run inside EHRs.

Here is a real MedicationRequest resource — note how it embeds SNOMED CT, references a patient, and carries structured dosage. Click the yellow numbered pins to reveal what each part means in pharmacy terms (or use the Show all notes button to display them all at once).

FHIR R4 — MedicationRequest (JSON)

Sticky notes — what JSON looks like
1
What is JSON? JSON ("JavaScript Object Notation") is just a way of writing structured data so computers can read it. Think of it like a paper prescription form translated into bullet points. The curly braces { } wrap one whole record. Inside, every line is a label : value pair, separated by commas. Square brackets [ ] hold a list. The labels in blue here are field names; the yellow text is the value. Reading FHIR JSON is exactly like reading a structured form — you don't need to write code.
2
"resourceType": "MedicationRequest" — every FHIR record begins by declaring what kind of resource it is. Here, it's a prescription order. Other examples you'll meet: Patient, Observation (a lab result), AllergyIntolerance (an allergy). FHIR has ~150 resource types, each with its own structured shape — like having a dedicated paper form for each kind of entry.
3
"status": "active" — pharmacist's lens: this tells you whether the order is currently in force. Common values: active (dispense it), completed (already dispensed and finished), stopped (discontinued by prescriber), cancelled, on-hold. Many medication errors come from systems that ignore status — never trust just the drug name.
4
"medicationCodeableConcept" — this whole block is the drug. "Codeable concept" is FHIR-speak for: "here is the meaning, expressed as a code from a known system, plus a human-readable name". A coding is like a triple receipt: the system (which dictionary?), the code (the number in that dictionary), and the display (the human-readable label).
5
"system": "...rxnorm" — this URL is FHIR's way of saying "the following code comes from the RxNorm dictionary". You don't need to visit the URL. It is just a unique identifier for the standard. http://snomed.info/sct means SNOMED CT; http://loinc.org means LOINC; http://unitsofmeasure.org means UCUM. Without a system, a code has no meaning — this is the most important safety rule in FHIR.
6
"code": "860975" — the actual RxCUI for "metformin hydrochloride 500 MG Extended Release Oral Tablet". You can verify this code yourself for free at rxnav.nlm.nih.gov. This single number replaces hundreds of inconsistent text strings ("Glucophage XR 500", "metformin HCl 500 ER", etc.) — they all map to 860975.
7
"subject": "Patient/JUH-998877" — this is FHIR's way of linking to another resource. The slash-separated path means "look up the Patient with id JUH-998877". Imagine each resource is a paper card in a filing cabinet with a unique number; references are how cards point to each other. The patient's name and date of birth live on the Patient card, not duplicated here.
8
"dosageInstruction" — the structured sig. The square brackets [ ] mean it's a list, because some prescriptions have multiple steps (e.g., "loading dose then maintenance"). Each entry in the list carries the human text and the machine-readable bits (timing, dose, route). Both must agree — exam scenarios often hinge on a free-text sig that contradicts the structured fields.
9
Timing — frequency: 1, period: 1, periodUnit: "d" — this is FHIR's structured way of writing "once daily". Read it as: "frequency 1 time per period of 1 day". For "twice daily" you'd see frequency: 2, period: 1, periodUnit: "d". For "every 8 hours": frequency: 1, period: 8, periodUnit: "h". Once you know the pattern, you can decode any FHIR timing in seconds.
10
Route uses SNOMED CT, not RxNorm — different jobs, different dictionaries! RxNorm names the drug; SNOMED CT names how it gets in (oral, IV, topical, etc.). The SCTID 26643006 is "Oral route". This layering is the whole point of the lecture: each standard does one job well, and FHIR carries them together in the same envelope.
11
"dispenseRequest" — this is the bit the pharmacy actually uses for dispensing: how many tablets to give and how many refills are allowed. numberOfRepeatsAllowed: 5 means 5 additional fills after the first dispense (so 6 fills total in some interpretations — always check your local profile). This is a frequent source of dispensing disputes.

Look at the layering: FHIR is the envelope, RxNorm codes the drug, SNOMED CT codes the route, UCUM codes the units. Every standard you just learned appears — doing the job it was designed for.

FHIR's killer feature for pharmacy is SMART on FHIR — apps that launch inside the EHR with a standard OAuth2 flow and read/write MedicationRequest, AllergyIntolerance, and Observation resources. Clinical decision support hooks (CDS Hooks) live here too.
CHECK 3 Map the task to the standard

A community pharmacy receives an e-prescription that includes an RxNorm code for the medication, a SNOMED CT code for "take with food" instructions, and a LOINC code for a pre-prescribing serum potassium level. The entire bundle is transmitted as a set of FHIR resources. Which standard is the transport?

Correct: D — FHIR. The other three are all content terminologies (meaning). FHIR is the transport format carrying them. Notice how in modern interoperability, the transport (the envelope) is rarely the interesting failure point — the meaning-layer terminologies inside it are what pharmacists actually work with daily.

Applied: How a Pharmacist Uses Interoperability Every Day

Five pharmacy workflows — and the exact standard that makes each one work (or fail).

Workflow 1 — Medication reconciliation at admission

Patient admitted from a community clinic to JUH. The resident pulls outside medication history. What just happened technically?

Community
EMR
→
HIE
→
JUH EHR

Standards at work:

  • Each medication normalised to an RxCUI so brand/generic variants collapse to one concept.
  • Allergies carry SNOMED CT codes for substance and manifestation.
  • Diagnoses carry ICD-11 for billing + SNOMED CT for clinical detail.
  • Exchange uses HL7 FHIR or HL7 CDA as a Continuity of Care Document (CCD).

Pharmacist's job: review reconciled list, flag duplicates the RxCUI match already caught, investigate why a drug appears twice if it does, and verify the allergy codes match reality.

Workflow 2 — Automated renal dose adjustment

A patient on enoxaparin has a new serum creatinine sent from an external lab. The EHR's dose-adjust rule needs to fire.

External lab
→
HL7 ORU^R01
→
EHR rule engine
→
CDS alert to pharmacist

For the rule to fire, every one of these must be correct:

  • OBX-3 = LOINC 2160-0 (not a local lab code) — semantic.
  • OBX-6 units in UCUM, matching the engine's configuration — structural.
  • Patient ID (PID-3) resolves to the same EHR patient — structural.
  • Interface engine delivered the message without dropping the OBX — foundational.

If any of these fail, the alert silently doesn't fire. This is why pharmacist informaticists review interface logs, not just clinical charts.

Workflow 3 — E-prescribing from clinic to community pharmacy

Prescriber EMR
→
NCPDP SCRIPT
NewRx
→
Pharmacy dispensing system

The NewRx message is XML. Inside the XML: the drug is encoded as RxNorm, the SIG is structured, the prescriber is identified. The pharmacy system validates all required elements; anything missing queues for manual review.

Real pharmacy pain-point: free-text SIGs that say "as directed" cannot be parsed into a structured dose + frequency, blocking automated dose-checking. This is a structural failure dressed in semantic clothing — the field exists, but its content is not computable.

Workflow 4 — Allergy alert at order entry

Prescriber tries to order amoxicillin for Ahmad Nabulsi, who has a documented penicillin allergy. The CPOE blocks the order. What allowed that?

  1. The allergy was coded as SNOMED CT 91936005 Allergy to penicillin (finding), with manifestation 39579001 Anaphylaxis and severity "severe".
  2. The ordered drug is RxNorm-coded. The system maps RxNorm → ingredient class (penicillins).
  3. The allergy engine checks: ordered ingredient class ⊇ documented allergic substance? → YES → fire severe interaction alert.

Notice how two different terminologies (SNOMED CT for the allergy, RxNorm for the drug) are bridged by class-level mapping. This is why rich coding matters: an allergy entered as free text "PCN" cannot participate in this check.

Workflow 5 — Patient's mobile app syncs to the EHR

Using a SMART-on-FHIR-enabled app, a diabetic patient's home blood glucose readings stream into the EHR as Observation resources with LOINC 2345-7. The pharmacist running the MTM clinic sees them in the same dashboard as in-clinic readings.

Every standard you've learned shows up:

  • FHIR Observation — the envelope and resource model;
  • LOINC 2345-7 — identifies "Glucose, serum";
  • UCUM mg/dL — the units;
  • OAuth2 + SMART — the security / organisational layer;
  • Patient-generated — so the dashboard badges it as PHR-origin, not provider-measured.

Putting It All Together

Your mental model for the exam — and for your first interface-engine meeting as a pharmacy informaticist.

🎯 The five things you must walk out of this lecture with

  • EMR ≠ EHR ≠ PHR — different scope, different control, different sharing.
  • Four levels — foundational, structural, semantic, organisational. Use the progressive question to locate any failure.
  • Content vs transport — terminologies carry meaning; messaging standards carry data. You need both.
  • The pharmacy toolkit — RxNorm (drugs), SNOMED CT (findings/ADRs), ICD (billing), LOINC (labs/observations), NCPDP SCRIPT (e-prescribing), HL7 v2 / CDA / FHIR (messaging).
  • Interoperability is a pharmacist-safety issue. Silent failures at any layer translate directly into medication errors.

🧭 Diagnostic decision tree — "which standard?"

A
Is it a drug?

→ Always RxNorm for the concept. (SNOMED CT has substance codes too, but RxNorm is the clinical drug standard.)

B
Is it an allergy, reaction, symptom, finding, or procedure?

→ SNOMED CT.

C
Is it a diagnosis for billing or reporting?

→ ICD-10/11. (Pair with SNOMED CT for clinical depth.)

D
Is it a lab test, vital sign, or clinical observation?

→ LOINC. Always paired with UCUM for units.

E
Is the question how the data gets there?

→ Transport: HL7 v2 (hospital legacy), HL7 CDA (documents), HL7 FHIR (modern APIs), NCPDP SCRIPT (e-prescribing).

🔍 Common exam traps to avoid

✕
Confusing semantic with structural

Units mismatch ≠ wrong code. Wrong code ≠ unreadable format. Read the stem twice.

✕
Assuming HL7 covers meaning

HL7 is a messaging standard. The meaning comes from the terminology codes inside the HL7 message.

✕
Treating PHR data as ground truth

Patient lists add signal but require reconciliation — not blind acceptance.

✕
Thinking EHR go-live = interoperable

Go-live is foundational at best. Semantic interoperability is a years-long mapping project.

§1.1 The three-letter soup: EMR · EHR · PHR
05

Three terms, three different worlds

They differ on who owns the record, how far it travels, and what it is allowed to do.

🏥
EMR — "My Record"

Digital paper chart used inside one organisation. Owned by the provider/facility. Rarely leaves intact.

scope: one org
🌐
EHR — "Everyone's Record"

Longitudinal record designed to be shared across organisations by authorised clinicians. Where interoperability lives.

scope: many orgs
📱
PHR — "Patient's Record"

Owned and controlled by the patient — usually an app or portal. Pharmacists reconcile rather than auto-trust.

scope: patient
Mnemonic: EMR = one org · EHR = everyone (with permission) · PHR = patient. If the question says "patient-entered" or "self-tracked", it's PHR. If it says "shared across settings", it's EHR.

§1.2 What is interoperability, really?
06

Three verbs that change everything

Interoperability — the ability of different information systems, devices, and applications to access, exchange, integrate, and cooperatively use data in a coordinated manner, within and across organisational, regional and national boundaries, to provide timely and seamless portability of information and optimise the health of individuals and populations. — adapted from HIMSS / ISO
①
Access

Can I retrieve the data I need from another system?

②
Exchange

Can two systems push and pull the data reliably?

③
Integrate & use

Can the receiver act on the data? Trigger alerts, run rules, calculate doses?

A hospital can exchange a PDF with a community pharmacy and still fail interoperability — because a PDF cannot be integrated and used by a dispensing system to trigger a duplicate-therapy alert.

§1.3 Pharmacist's 4 transition risks
07

When interoperability fails, patients are harmed

These four are not theoretical — they happen weekly in any hospital with weak interoperability.

1
Duplicate therapy

"Glucophage XR 500 mg" and "Metformin HCl 500 mg ER" arrive as two different text strings — the system treats them as two drugs and the patient ends up on accidental double-dosing.

2
Missed allergy alerts

"Allergic to PCN" as free text cannot match penicillin orders at the ADR rule level. The CPOE never fires; the order goes through.

3
Wrong renal dose

Outside-lab serum creatinine delayed or with mismatched units — eGFR falls out of automated dose-adjust calculators. Aminoglycosides, DOACs and metformin all affected.

4
Stuck e-prescriptions

NewRx arrives with a missing field; the Rx queues for manual fix, delaying dispensing and frustrating the patient at the counter.

SECTION 2 The Four Levels of Interoperability
09
02

A ladder, not a menu

Each rung answers a different question — and each rung can fail independently.

  • Foundational — can we exchange data at all?
  • Structural — can the receiver parse it?
  • Semantic — do we agree on what it means?
  • Organisational — are we permitted to do this?
A "no" at any rung tells you exactly where the problem lives. Use these in order.
Dr. Samar J. Melhem, PhD · Informatics in Pharmacy Practice · Week 10 · Faculty of Pharmacy, University of Jordan

§2.1 Progressive questions, progressive levels
10

Walk the ladder in order

1
Foundational — "Can we exchange data at all?"

Basic secure connectivity. A web service answers; a file transfer completes. No guarantee the bytes are meaningful yet.

2
Structural — "Can the receiver parse it?"

Agreed message format, syntax, data types, units. HL7 v2 segments, XML schemas, JSON profiles, UCUM units all live here.

3
Semantic — "Do we agree on what it means?"

Shared terminologies guarantee that "serum creatinine" means the same test and "penicillin allergy" means the same substance + reaction on both sides.

4
Organisational — "Are we permitted to do this, and who governs it?"

Consent, policy, legal framework, trust agreements, data-use agreements, governance committees — the non-technical layer that decides whether the exchange is allowed.

Sticky notes — TLS and UCUM
1
TLS = "Transport Layer Security". The protocol that secures every healthcare data exchange — the same "🔒 padlock" you see in your browser. It guarantees three things at once: encryption (no one in the middle can read the message), authentication (the sender and receiver are who they claim to be — checked via digital certificates), and integrity (no one altered the message in flight). If any of the three fails, the channel refuses to open. This is why an expired TLS certificate is a foundational-level interoperability incident — even with everything else perfect, the message cannot move.
2
UCUM = "Unified Code for Units of Measure". The international standard that assigns one canonical machine-readable string to every unit of measurement (mg/dL, mmol/L, kg, mL, %, etc.). UCUM is case-sensitive: mg/dL is correct; MG/DL and mg/dl are not. UCUM is the partner standard to LOINC: LOINC tells you what is being measured, UCUM tells you in what units. A LOINC code without a UCUM unit is meaningless.

CHECK 2 Where does this failure live?
12

Locating an interoperability failure

Q · 02 Diagnose the rung

A lab result arrives at the hospital EHR. The LOINC code is correct (2160-0). However, the sending system used units of µmol/L while the hospital configured its alert engine for mg/dL. The "critically high" alerts never fire.

Correct: B — Structural. The semantic layer agrees (same test, LOINC 2160-0). The breakdown is in representation: how the numeric value and its units are packaged. Fix = enforce UCUM units at the interface and add a unit-conversion step. This is why integration testing must validate not only codes but units, reference ranges, precision and datatypes.

Sticky notes — PA and JSON
PA
PA = "Prior Authorisation" (also called pre-authorisation). The insurer's approval that must be obtained before dispensing certain medications, services, or procedures — otherwise the claim is rejected. Used in Jordan by RMS, the Civil Insurance Programme, and private insurers (MetLife, Allianz, Mednet, Globemed) for biologics, oncology, GLP-1s, ADHD medications, and most chronic-disease drugs above the formulary threshold. Like a paper form translated into bullet points: curly braces { } wrap a record, and inside, every line is a label : value pair separated by commas; square brackets [ ] hold a list. §3.2 Terminology #1 — RxNorm
15

RxNorm — medication normalisation

RxNorm — a standardised nomenclature for clinical drugs maintained by the U.S. National Library of Medicine. It assigns each medication concept an RxCUI (RxNorm Concept Unique Identifier) and links brand names, generics, strengths, and dose forms to one concept.

The problem RxNorm solves — same drug, three strings

Before RxNorm
# Pharmacy A sends: "Glucophage XR 500mg extended-release tablet" # Hospital LIS sends: "Metformin hydrochloride 500mg ER tablet" # Clinic EMR sends: "Metformin HCl 500mg XR tab" # Receiving system: treats them as 3 different medications ⚠️1
Sticky note
1
Why this is dangerous in pharmacy. If three records refer to the same molecule but the system thinks they are three different drugs, the patient appears to be on three concurrent metformin prescriptions. The duplicate-therapy alert should fire — but it cannot, because the system never recognises them as duplicates. This is exactly how patients end up on accidental double-dosing. The fix is to give every drug one canonical identifier.

§3.2 RxNorm — One RxCUI, four term types
16

After normalisation — one RxCUI

After RxNorm
RxCUI2: 860975 # metformin hydrochloride 500 MG ER Oral Tablet TTY3: SCD # Semantic Clinical Drug Name: "metformin hydrochloride 500 MG Extended Release Oral Tablet" # All 3 strings → same RxCUI → duplicate-therapy alert fires ✓
Sticky notes
2
RxCUI = "RxNorm Concept Unique Identifier" — a number that names a drug concept unambiguously, the way an ISBN names a book. 860975 always means the same thing, in any system that uses RxNorm. Free, maintained by the US National Library of Medicine.
3
TTY = "Term Type". RxNorm tags every concept with its level of specificity, so you know whether you're looking at just an ingredient, a generic clinical drug, or a branded product. The five you'll meet most often: IN ingredient (e.g., metformin alone), SCDC ingredient + strength, SCDF ingredient + form, SCD ingredient + strength + form (generic — the pharmacy normalisation target), SBD same but branded, BN just the brand name. Hierarchy: IN → SCDC/SCDF → SCD → SBD ← BN. Use SCD for medication reconciliation, IN for duplicate-therapy and allergy checks, SBD for brand-specific dispensing. Caution: in non-RxNorm contexts (e.g., patient-information leaflets) "TTY" can mean "Teletypewriter" — a hearing-impaired-assistance device. Different field, same letters.

RxNorm Term Types — the ones pharmacists actually meet

IN
Ingredient

The active moiety alone. Metformin.

SCD
Semantic Clinical Drug

Ingredient + strength + form (generic). Metformin 500 MG ER Oral Tablet.

SBD
Semantic Branded Drug

Brand + strength + form. Glucophage XR 500 MG Oral Tablet.

BN
Brand Name

The trade name only. Glucophage.

§3.3 Terminology #2 — SNOMED CT
17

SNOMED CT — comprehensive clinical terminology

SNOMED CT (Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine — Clinical Terms) is the world's most comprehensive clinical terminology. It covers clinical findings, disorders, procedures, body structures, organisms, substances, and relationships — over 350,000 concepts.
SNOMED CT — Allergy to penicillin
Concept ID1: 91936005 FSN2: "Allergy to penicillin (finding)" Preferred: "Penicillin allergy" # Relationships: Is-a3 → 416098002 "Drug allergy (finding)" Causative agent4 → 373270004 "Penicillin -class of antibiotic-"
Sticky notes — SNOMED CT structure
1
Concept ID = SCTID — every SNOMED concept has a permanent number. 91936005 means "Allergy to penicillin" forever, in every language. The number itself has no meaning — but the structure of the concept is described by its relationships.
2
FSN = "Fully Specified Name". The unambiguous English name. The text in brackets — (finding) — is the semantic tag: finding, disorder, procedure, substance, body structure, etc. Penicillin the molecule is also in SNOMED but tagged (substance) with a different SCTID. Always read the bracketed tag.
3
"Is-a" = the parent in the hierarchy. Penicillin allergy is-a drug allergy is-a hypersensitivity reaction is-a finding. This hierarchy is what allows a query "show all drug allergies" to retrieve every kind of drug allergy automatically.
4
"Causative agent" is a relationship that links this allergy concept to what causes it. This is what enables a smart pharmacy alert: when you order amoxicillin (an RxNorm drug), the system maps amoxicillin to its SNOMED substance concept, walks up to "penicillin -class of antibiotic-" via SNOMED relationships, and recognises the patient's documented allergy applies.

§3.3 SNOMED CT in everyday pharmacy
18

How a pharmacist uses SNOMED CT today

🚨
Coding allergies & ADRs

Substance + reaction manifestation + severity as coded concepts — not free text. This is the only way an allergy alert engine can fire reliably across drug classes.

📋
Structured clinical findings

"Nausea", "dyspnoea", "wheeze" — each has an SCTID that travels safely between systems. Free-text fields lose their meaning at every interface.

🧪
Procedures & interventions

"Medication review", "INR monitoring", "MUR consultation" — pharmacist activities coded for service reporting and reimbursement.

🔗
Cross-system meaning

The same SCTID in the hospital, the clinic, and the community pharmacy means the same thing — by design. No fuzzy matching needed.

If the scenario describes an allergic reaction, adverse drug reaction, symptom, finding, or clinical procedure — SNOMED CT is usually the right terminology. Free SNOMED CT browser at browser.ihtsdotools.org.

§3.4 Terminology #3 — LOINC
19

LOINC — labs, vitals, observations

LOINC (Logical Observation Identifiers Names and Codes) provides a universal code system for identifying laboratory tests, clinical measurements, and other observations. Maintained by the Regenstrief Institute. Names a test by six formal axes: Component, Property, Timing, System, Scale, Method.
LOINC 2160-0 — fully specified
LOINC code: 2160-01 Long name: "Creatinine [Mass/volume] in Serum or Plasma" # Six-axis specification:2 Component: Creatinine Property: MCnc # Mass concentration Time: Pt # Point in time System: Ser/Plas3 # Serum or plasma Scale: Qn # Quantitative Example UCUM units4: mg/dL # or µmol/L
Sticky notes — LOINC fields
1
The LOINC code 2160-0 is just an identifier. The number-dash-number format is LOINC's signature; everything before the dash is the actual code, the digit after the dash is a check digit (catches typos). You don't need to memorise the number — just recognise the format.
2
The "six axes" are LOINC's clever idea: instead of a vague name, each test is described along six dimensions — what is being measured, how, when, on what specimen, scale, method. Two tests that look identical but differ on even one axis get different codes. A urine creatinine and a serum creatinine cannot be confused.
3
The "System" axis = the specimen. Ser/Plas = serum or plasma; Urine = urine; Bld = whole blood. Most important for safety. A renal-dose adjustment algorithm written for serum creatinine MUST refuse to act on urine creatinine — the numbers are completely different.
4
UCUM = "Unified Code for Units of Measure". Partner standard to LOINC: LOINC says what is being measured, UCUM says in what units. UCUM is case-sensitive: mg/dL is correct; mg/dl, MG/DL, Mg/dL are all rejected by conformant systems.

§3.6 Transport #1 — HL7 v2 ORU^R01
21

Reading a real HL7 v2 lab message

Click each yellow pin to decode the part. HL7 v2 is just a sentence with strict punctuation.

MSH1|^~\&2|JUH_LAB|JUH|EHR|JUH|2026042410303||ORU^R014|MSG0001|P|2.5.1 PID5|1||JUH-9988776||Nabulsi^Ahmad^M7||19580612|M OBR8|1||LAB2026-0456|2160-0^Creatinine^LN OBX9|1|NM10|2160-0^Creatinine^LN||1.8211|mg/dL12|0.7-1.3|H13||F
Sticky notes — reading HL7 v2
1
Segments are the lines. Every HL7 v2 message is a stack of lines, each starting with a 3-letter code: MSH = Message Header, PID = Patient ID, OBR = Observation Request (what was ordered), OBX = Observation Result (the answer).
2
The funny characters |^~\& are HL7's punctuation, declared up front: | = field separator, ^ = component separator (smaller than a field), ~ = repetition, \ = escape, & = sub-component.
3
Timestamp 202604241030 = 2026-04-24, 10:30. HL7 v2 uses one continuous string of digits with no separators: YYYYMMDDHHMM.
4
ORU^R01 = the message type. ORU = "Observational Result, Unsolicited" — the lab pushes the result without being asked. Other types: ADT^A01 (admission), RDS^O13 (pharmacy dispense), OMP^O09 (pharmacy order).
5
PID = Patient Identification. Everything on this line is about who the patient is. There is exactly one PID per patient. Mismatched PID and OBX = right result attached to wrong patient — the most dangerous error in clinical messaging.
6
JUH-998877 is Ahmad's hospital MRN — Jordan University Hospital's local identifier. In a real message this also carries the assigning authority and identifier type (MR, NI, etc.). Two hospitals can have MRN 998877 meaning two different people.
7
Nabulsi^Ahmad^M — caret separates name components: family^given^middle. Reads as: family "Nabulsi", given "Ahmad", middle initial "M".
8
OBR = Observation Request. What was ordered — here, a serum creatinine identified by LOINC 2160-0. One OBR per ordered test.
9
OBX = Observation Result. Where the actual result lives. One OBX per result. A complete metabolic panel has ~14 OBX segments — one per analyte.
10
NM = numeric value. Other common types: ST (string text), CE/CWE (coded entry, e.g. cultures), SN (structured numeric, like "<0.1").
11
1.82 — the numeric result. Always read it together with the next two fields (units and reference range). A number on its own is meaningless.
12
mg/dL — must be a valid UCUM unit (case-sensitive). The downstream renal-dose engine string-matches this token; one wrong character and the alert silently fails to fire.
13
H = High abnormal flag. Other values: L (low), HH (critically high), LL (critically low), N (normal). For a pharmacist, the H flag is the trigger to check the patient's medication list for nephrotoxic or renally-excreted drugs.
Dr. Samar J. Melhem, PhD · Informatics in Pharmacy Practice · Week 10 · Faculty of Pharmacy, University of Jordan

22

NCPDP SCRIPT — the e-prescribing standard

NCPDP SCRIPT — the National Council for Prescription Drug Programs standard for electronic prescribing between prescribers and pharmacies. XML-based. Defines: NewRx, RxRenewal, RxChange, CancelRx, RxFill, RxHistory.

A complete NewRx maps to the "five rights" + metadata

👤
Patient

Name, DOB, address, phone, gender, identifier

💊
Medication

RxNorm-coded drug, strength, form, quantity, days supply, refills

📋
Directions (SIG)

Structured SIG — verb, dose, route, frequency, indication

👨‍⚕️
Prescriber

Name, DEA/NPI or equivalent, contact, signature

🏪
Pharmacy

Recipient identifier (NCPDP ID or equivalent)

🕐
Metadata

Date written, message IDs, DUR screening flags

When you see a scenario where "the e-prescription arrives missing a field" — that's NCPDP SCRIPT, and the problem is structural interoperability. The semantic layer may be fine; the envelope is incomplete.

§3.8 Transport #3 — HL7 FHIR
23

FHIR MedicationRequest — the modern envelope

FHIR R4 — JSON
{ "resourceType": "MedicationRequest"1, "status": "active"2, "medicationCodeableConcept": { "coding": [{ "system": "http://www.nlm.nih.gov/research/umls/rxnorm"3, "code": "860975", "display": "metformin 500 MG ER Oral Tablet" }] }, "subject": { "reference": "Patient/JUH-998877"4 }, "dosageInstruction": [{ "text": "Take 1 tablet by mouth once daily", "timing": { "repeat": { "frequency": 1, "period": 1, "periodUnit": "d" } }5, "route": { "coding": [{ "system": "http://snomed.info/sct"6, "code": "26643006", "display": "Oral route" }] } }] }
Sticky notes — reading FHIR JSON
1
resourceType — every FHIR record begins by declaring what kind of resource it is. Here, it's a prescription order. Other examples: Patient, Observation, AllergyIntolerance. FHIR has ~150 resource types.
2
status: "active" — pharmacist's lens: this tells you whether the order is in force. Common values: active, completed, stopped, cancelled, on-hold. Many medication errors come from systems that ignore status.
3
system + code + display = a "codeable concept". The system URL declares which dictionary the code comes from (RxNorm here). Without a system, a code has no meaning — the most important safety rule in FHIR.
4
"subject": "Patient/JUH-998877" — FHIR's way of linking to another resource. Each resource is a paper card in a filing cabinet with a unique number; references are how cards point to each other.
5
Timing — frequency 1, period 1, periodUnit "d" = "once daily". For "twice daily": frequency 2. For "every 8 hours": frequency 1, period 8, periodUnit "h".
6
Route uses SNOMED CT, not RxNorm — different jobs, different dictionaries! RxNorm names the drug; SNOMED CT names how it gets in. The whole point: each standard does one job well, FHIR carries them together.
FHIR is the envelope; RxNorm codes the drug; SNOMED CT codes the route; UCUM codes the units. Every standard you learned appears together — doing the job it was designed for.

Knowledge check — Content vs transport

Q · 03 Identify the transport

A community pharmacy receives an e-prescription that includes an RxNorm code for the medication, a SNOMED CT code for "take with food" instructions, and a LOINC code for a pre-prescribing serum potassium level. The entire bundle is transmitted as a set of FHIR resources. Which standard is the transport?

Correct: D — FHIR. The other three are all content terminologies (meaning). FHIR is the transport format carrying them. In modern interoperability, the transport (the envelope) is rarely the interesting failure point — the meaning-layer terminologies inside it are what pharmacists work with daily.

How a pharmacist uses interoperability every day

1
Med reconciliation at admission

RxCUI collapses brand/generic variants. Allergies in SNOMED CT. Diagnoses in ICD-11. Exchange via FHIR or HL7 CDA / CCD.

2
Automated renal dose adjustment

HL7 ORU^R01 with LOINC 2160-0 + UCUM mg/dL. Break any one and the dose-adjust rule never fires — pharmacist informaticist reads interface logs.

3
E-prescribing → community pharmacy

NCPDP SCRIPT NewRx in XML. Drug = RxNorm. SIG structured. Free-text "as directed" = silent structural failure that blocks dose-checking.

4
Allergy alert at order entry

SNOMED CT 91936005 (allergy) + manifestation + severity. Order RxNorm-coded → maps to ingredient class (penicillins) → CPOE blocks.

5
Patient mobile app syncs to EHR (SMART on FHIR)

Home glucose readings stream as FHIR Observation resources with LOINC 2345-7 + UCUM mg/dL. OAuth2 + SMART handles auth. Dashboard badges them as PHR-origin, not provider-measured. Every standard you learned shows up at once.

Common exam traps

✕
Semantic vs structural

Units mismatch ≠ wrong code. Read the stem twice.

✕
HL7 covers meaning?

No — HL7 is transport. Meaning lives in the codes inside.

✕
PHR = ground truth?

No — reconcile, don't auto-trust.

✕
Go-live = interoperable?

No — go-live is foundational at best.

🎯 Five things you must walk out with

  • EMR ≠ EHR ≠ PHR — different scope, control, sharing.
  • Four levels — foundational, structural, semantic, organisational.
  • Content vs transport — terminologies carry meaning, messaging carries data.
  • Pharmacy toolkit — RxNorm · SNOMED CT · ICD · LOINC · NCPDP SCRIPT · HL7 v2 / CDA / FHIR.
  • Interoperability is a pharmacist-safety issue — silent failures = medication errors.
